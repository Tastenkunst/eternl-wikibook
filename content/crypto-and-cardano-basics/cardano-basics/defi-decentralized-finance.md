# Decentralized Finance Explained

Decentralized finance, often shortened to **DeFi**, describes financial services built using blockchains and smart contracts. Depending on the application, you can exchange tokens, provide liquidity, borrow assets or use other financial services without relying on a traditional financial institution to execute every transaction.

DeFi brings together open networks, digital assets and programmable rules. It offers a foundation for financial services that can be used across borders and for new ways to exchange and manage assets. Different applications make different design choices and carry different risks.

## The Vision of DeFi

DeFi aims to make financial tools more accessible and give people more direct control over their assets. Someone with an internet connection and a suitable wallet can potentially interact with services without opening a traditional bank account. For people who face barriers to conventional financial services, this could create new opportunities.

Public blockchains also allow people to inspect transactions and, where available, the code and rules behind applications. Developers can build on existing protocols to create new services. Openness alone does not ensure safety or equal access, but it offers a foundation for a more transparent and inclusive financial system.

## How DeFi Works

A DeFi application combines a user interface with rules implemented in smart contracts. On Cardano, those rules can control how blockchain outputs are spent. You use a wallet to interact with the application and approve the transactions you want to submit.

For example, a decentralized exchange can use smart contracts to facilitate token swaps. A lending application can use them to manage deposits, loans and collateral. The user interface helps you interact with these contracts, but the interface and other services around it may still be operated by a team or company.

Connecting your wallet to an application does not by itself authorize every future transaction. Review each signature request and transaction before approving it. A smart contract cannot read your seed phrase or spending password, and a legitimate application should never ask you to enter your seed phrase to connect.

## Common Uses

- **Swapping:** Exchange one token for another through a decentralized exchange.
- **Providing liquidity:** Deposit assets into a liquidity pool so others can trade against it. Liquidity providers may earn a share of fees, but their returns and the value of their deposit are not guaranteed.
- **Lending and borrowing:** Supply assets or borrow against collateral. Borrowing can result in liquidation if the value of the collateral falls or other conditions are met.
- **Stablecoins:** Use tokens designed to track a reference value such as a fiat currency. Their ability to maintain that value depends on their design and is not guaranteed.

You can learn more about exchanges and marketplaces in [DEXes & Marketplaces](/crypto-and-cardano-basics/cardano-basics/dexes-and-marketplaces.md).

## Other DeFi Concepts

**Yield farming** generally means using assets in DeFi protocols to seek returns, which may include fees, interest or additional token incentives. **Liquidity mining** is a form of incentive in which participants receive tokens for supplying liquidity. The terms are used differently across projects; neither implies a guaranteed return.

Some protocols issue **governance tokens** that can give holders a say in certain decisions about that protocol. These are separate from Cardano's on-chain governance. The rights associated with a token depend on the project.

DeFi applications can also use tokenized representations of other assets and, in some cases, services that connect different blockchains. Such arrangements depend on their design and can introduce additional risks, including reliance on issuers, custodians or bridge software.

## Risks to Consider

Smart contracts can contain errors, and even an audited application can have vulnerabilities. Applications may also depend on external services, price feeds or administrative controls that introduce additional risks.

Token prices can change quickly, liquidity may be limited, and a token can imitate the name or appearance of another token. When providing liquidity, you may end up with a different mix of assets and a lower value than if you had simply held them. Stablecoins can lose their intended peg, and borrowed positions may be liquidated.

Legal and regulatory requirements can also differ by location and service, and they can change over time.

Check the application and token carefully, understand what you are approving, and use only funds you can afford to put at risk. For more general precautions, see [Security Best Practices](/crypto-and-cardano-basics/security-and-privacy/Security-Best-Practices.md).
